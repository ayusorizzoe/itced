Ossuary

product research conceptual experimental

Everything the body becomes eventually fits in a container. Ossuary starts there.


Ossuary is a ceramic collection of functional objects, ashtrays, jewelry trays, candle holders, and a vase, whose forms draw from the morphology of human bone. Shaped like spines, ribs, and skeletal fragments, the pieces sit at the intersection of the anatomical and the domestic. The collection is coherent in form and exploratory in finish, treating glaze as a second layer of research running parallel to the making.

The name came first. An ossuary is a container for the bones of the dead. An ashtray is a container for what burning leaves behind. These are objects that hold residue in the shape of what the body becomes.

The formal departure point was bone morphology, though not as anatomical precision. The shapes are intuitive interpretations rather than scientific reproductions, bone-like in feeling and proportion rather than in clinical accuracy. The project required substantial investigation into ceramic technique alongside this: glaze chemistry, slip casting, mold making, and firing behavior shaped every decision from form to finish.



The slip cast pieces were digitally modeled in Blender and 3D printed to produce the molds, before being cast in clay and fired. The forms that read as organic, bodily, and handmade were constructed computationally before they ever became ceramic. That passage is worth naming.

The collection is unified by form and open in finish. Glaze was treated as its own experimental territory: different recipes, different applications, different results across pieces. Some surfaces are deep black and glossy, reading almost industrial. Others are matte white with bronze-toned markings that pool and branch like vascular tissue. The green and gold piece carries a texture closer to mineral than body. None of the glazes were predetermined solutions. They were tests, and the collection carries that openly.

The spine ashtrays anchor the series as its most resolved forms. The vase, hand-built rather than cast, stands apart in both technique and scale. Its thorn-covered surface pushes the bone reference toward something rawer and less domestic.

The objects are functional. The ashtrays receive ash, the jewelry trays hold small things, the candle holders hold light, the vase holds stems. These are not sculptural objects borrowing the language of function. They are functional products that happen to be shaped like the inside of a body. That combination is where the collection finds its place in this world.
